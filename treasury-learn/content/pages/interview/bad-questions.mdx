## Why these questions fail

Three failure modes cover almost every bad interview question (the categories follow common customer-discovery teaching[](cite:mom-test,torres-wrong-questions)):

1. **Hypothetical / future** — "Would you…?", "Will you…?". People predict their future behaviour badly and are polite about your idea. The answer is an opinion, not evidence.
2. **General / habitual** — "How do you usually…?", "What's your biggest problem?". The answer is the idealised process or the most recent complaint, not what happens.
3. **Leading / about you** — the question contains the answer, or invites a compliment. Treasurers are courteous professionals; many will tell a founder what the founder evidently wants to hear.

Treasury adds two more:

4. **Sensitive** — questions that sound like fraud reconnaissance or ask for confidential data. They end interviews or make the rest guarded.
5. **Naive** — questions that reveal you haven't done basic homework (e.g. confusing treasury with accounting). They cost credibility and time. See [Vocabulary](/interview/vocabulary).

## Three questions that look reasonable

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| **"Would you use AI for cash forecasting?"** | Hypothetical, and "AI" means something different to everyone. You get either polite enthusiasm or a generic compliance worry — neither tells you what they do. It also turns the interview into a pitch. | "Walk me through the last forecast you produced. Which part took longest? Which numbers did you change by hand, and what did you know that the system didn't?" |
| **"Would you pay for X?"** | People say yes to be kind, and the interviewee is often not the budget holder. A friendly "yes" in an interview is a weak signal; past spending and effort are much stronger evidence[](cite:mom-test). | "What have you spent money or time on to fix this so far — tools, consultants, headcount, internal builds? Who approved it? What happened the last time someone proposed a new treasury tool?" |
| **"Is this a good startup idea?"** | Asks for a compliment and an opinion outside their expertise. Treasurers know their work, not venture outcomes. | Don't ask. If you want their view on importance: "Of the things you did this month, which would hurt most if it went wrong? Which took the most time?" |

## Hypothetical and future questions

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Would a real-time cash dashboard be useful?" | Everyone says yes to "useful". Tells you nothing about whether they'd change behaviour. | "When did you last need a balance you couldn't see? What did you do instead, and what did it cost?" |
| "Would you trust an automated system to execute FX hedges?" | Abstract; answers reflect mood about automation, not their controls. | "Walk me through the last hedge. Which steps were rules, and which were your call? What would you never let a system do there — and why?" |
| "How much time would you save if…?" | Guessing about a counterfactual. | "How long did last week's forecast take, start to finish? How much of that was waiting for others?" |
| "If you had a magic wand…?" | Produces wishes — often a better version of the tool they already have. | "What did you last build yourself — a macro, template, report — and why did you have to?" |

## General and habitual questions

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "How do you do cash forecasting?" | Gets the process as documented, not as practised. | "Walk me through the last forecast you produced — where did the first number come from?" |
| "What are your biggest pain points?" | Gets the most recent annoyance or a conference talking point ("data quality", "visibility"). | "Tell me about the last time something took much longer than it should have." · "What happened the last time the forecast was badly wrong?" |
| "Do you have any problems with bank connectivity?" | Yes/no, and prompts a generic complaint. | "Which bank statements arrived late or incomplete last month? What did you do about it?" |
| "How accurate is your forecast?" | Invites a number without definition — "about 90%" means nothing without horizon, measure and lines. | "When did you last compare the forecast with actuals? What was the biggest miss, and what caused it?" |
| "What does a typical day look like?" | "Typical" smooths out the exceptions — which are the work ([why "yesterday" beats "a typical day"](why:yesterday-beats-typical-day)). | "What did you do yesterday, from when you logged in?" · "What broke your last normal Monday?" |

## Leading and compliment-fishing questions

Leading questions are the most expensive failure mode here: a courteous treasurer will confirm your premise whether or not it is true, and you cannot tell the difference afterwards — [why the open past-instance form beats it](why:open-vs-leading).

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Forecasting in Excel is really painful, isn't it?" | Contains the answer; they will agree. | "What do you use for forecasting, and what do you do in Excel specifically?" — then let them volunteer the pain, or not. |
| "Don't you find subsidiaries' forecasts unreliable?" | Leading, and invites a complaint about colleagues. | "Which entity's numbers did you change last time, and why?" |
| "We're building a tool that does X — what do you think?" | Turns the interview into a pitch; they review your idea politely. | Keep the idea out entirely during discovery[](cite:mom-test). If they ask what you're working on, answer honestly in one sentence at the end. |
| "Treasury is going to be transformed by AI — how are you preparing?" | Loaded premise; puts them on the defensive or invites buzzwords. | "What changed in your job in the last 2–3 years? What's new that you didn't do before?" |

## Sensitive questions (treasury-specific)

These don't just produce bad data — they can end the conversation, because *specifics* about payment controls resemble the reconnaissance fraudsters do before a [business email compromise](term:business-email-compromise) or CEO-fraud attempt[](cite:bacs-ceo-fraud-2026).

<Callout type="synthesis" title="The boundary — drawn once">

Fraud controls and approval authority are **core discovery topics**. How a supplier bank-detail change gets verified, who has to approve what, what gets escalated and how the thresholds are written down — ask about all of it, as open questions about the last instance. What is off-limits is the **specific values** that make an attack work or breach confidentiality: limit amounts, signatory names, call-back numbers (plus account numbers, balances, counterparties, documents and loss details). And the phrasing matters: never ask a **leading** question that describes their live control back to them, and never drop jargon you can't back up — both read as reconnaissance or fake expertise rather than curiosity. [Why this line, and not a ban on the topic](why:discovery-vs-reconnaissance)

</Callout>

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "What's your approval limit in CHF — and who are the signatories?" | Asks for the values behind the control: exactly what a fraudster needs, and confidential authority data. The *topic* is fine; the *numbers and names* are not. | "Roughly who has to approve what — and is it written down or a judgment call?" (authority and escalation: fine. The amounts and names: leave them out.) |
| "So when a supplier sends new bank details, someone calls them back on a number you already have, right?" | Leading: describes a live control back to them and buys a polite "yes" — and tells any suspicious listener how much you know. | "Without the specifics — walk me through what happened the last time a supplier's bank details changed." |
| "Which banks do you use for which entities?" | Bank relationships are confidential and useful to attackers. | "Roughly how many banks and accounts — tens or hundreds? How many different e-banking logins do you use?" |
| "Can you send me your forecast template / a screenshot?" | Asks for internal documents; may breach policy. | "Describe the template — what are the rows and columns, roughly? Who fills in which part?" Let them offer documents; don't request them. |
| "How much cash do you have / what are you paying on your RCF?" | Confidential (unless published); irrelevant to workflow discovery. | Use public information from the annual report if needed; ask about the process, not the numbers. |
| "Are you planning a refinancing?" | Potential inside information at listed companies. | "Walk me through the last refinancing, as far as you can talk about it." |

<Example title="The safe version of the bank-details question, in practice" open>

> **You:** "Without the specifics — the last time a supplier's bank details changed, what happened?"
> **AP:** "The request came by email. I flagged it, somebody called the supplier on the number we already had, and the change went through a second approval before the next run."
> **You:** "Who noticed it first — and what changed afterwards?"
> **AP:** "I did. Now anything like that comes to me and the controller by default."

You get the detection point, the sequence, the roles and a process change — enough to map the workflow — without a single number, name or procedure that belongs in an attacker's notes. The interviewee set the level of detail; that is the sign you asked it right.

</Example>

## Naive questions (credibility cost)

| Bad question | Why it fails | Ask instead / know first |
|---|---|---|
| "So you do the bookkeeping and the budget?" | Confuses treasury with accounting and FP&A. | Know the difference ([Treasury and its neighbours](/start/neighbours)); ask "Where does your work stop and accounting's begin?" |
| "What's a 13-week forecast?" | Basic term; signals you haven't prepared. | Know it ([short-term forecast](term:short-term-forecast)); ask "Why 13 weeks here — and what happens beyond it?" |
| "Why don't you just automate the payments?" | Implies they haven't thought of it; payments are often highly automated already, and the human steps are controls. | "Which steps in the payment process are deliberately manual, and why?" |
| "Does the CFO do all this?" (to a Group Treasurer) | Misreads the organisation at that size. | "Which decisions do you take, and which go to the CFO?" |
| "What's your TMS?" as the opener | Tool-first; frames the conversation as a software evaluation. | Start with the work; the tools appear naturally in the walk-through. |
| "So you load the camt.054s into your TMS every morning?" | Dropped jargon you haven't checked — doubly wrong here: the daily statement is MT940/[camt.053](term:camt-053) (camt.054 is the item detail behind a booking), and many mid-market groups have no TMS at all. | Know the terms ([Vocabulary](/interview/vocabulary)); ask "how does bank data get into your systems today?" |

<Callout type="interview" title="If you catch yourself asking a bad question">

Recover by turning it into a request for a specific past instance: "Actually — let me ask that differently. When did that last happen?" Interviewees rarely mind; most prefer telling a story to giving an opinion.

</Callout>
