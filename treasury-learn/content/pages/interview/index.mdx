## What this section is for

The goal of a discovery interview is to learn **what a specific person actually did, recently, in a specific workflow** — the systems they opened, the data they pulled, the people they chased, the judgment they applied and what happened afterwards. It is not to test an idea, and it is not a sales meeting. Everything here is built around that goal.

<Callout type="concept" title="The method in three sentences">

Customer-discovery practice converges on a few rules. Talk about **their** work, not your idea; ask about **specific past events**, not opinions about the future; and **listen more than you talk**[](cite:mom-test). People are unreliable when answering direct, general questions ("what matters most to you?"), but fairly reliable when retelling a specific recent story, which the interviewer helps "excavate" step by step[](cite:torres-wrong-questions,torres-story-based). Your beliefs about the customer's problem are hypotheses to test outside the office, starting with the problem before any solution[](cite:blank-customer-development).

</Callout>

For treasury this translates into a very concrete pattern: *"Walk me through the last cash forecast you produced"* rather than *"How do you do cash forecasting?"*, and never *"Would you use a tool that…?"*.

## Where to find treasury people

Treasury is a small, well-networked profession. Most practitioners belong to a national association, attend one or two events a year, and are reachable on LinkedIn. Channels, roughly from most to least productive for a founder in Switzerland/DACH (field note, not a measured ranking):

| Channel | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm introductions** | Investors, your own CFO, auditors, fiduciaries, former colleagues now in finance | Highest acceptance. Ask the introducer for the *person who does the work*, not just the CFO they know. |
| **Swiss associations** | **SwissACT** (Swiss Association of Corporate Treasurers), represented at the Swiss Treasury Summit[](cite:hslu-swiss-treasury-summit); **SwissTreasurer / ACTSR** in French-speaking Switzerland, which holds a forum and AGM in Geneva each June[](cite:swisstreasurer-forum-2026,eact-nta); the UK ACT's **Treasury Network Switzerland** holds networking and careers events in Zurich[](cite:act-tn-switzerland) | Membership is mostly practitioners. Events are small enough that a thoughtful question gets noticed. |
| **Swiss Treasury Summit** | Annual conference run by HSLU's IFZ in Zug-Rotkreuz (15 Sept 2026 edition); gathers graduates of the CAS Swiss Certified Treasurer programme, practitioners, CFOs and SwissACT; active treasurers and CFOs get a 50% discount, service providers pay full price[](cite:hslu-swiss-treasury-summit) | The single densest Swiss gathering of treasurers. Alumni of the CAS programme are also findable on LinkedIn by the credential. |
| **Germany / Austria** | **VDT** (Verband Deutscher Treasurer): 2,000+ members from almost 900 companies, specialist working groups and regional networks, Treasury Day in Frankfurt[](cite:vdt-home). Austria: **ACTA** and ÖPWZ Forum Finanzen[](cite:eact-nta) | VDT working groups (cash & liquidity, risk, debt) are where practitioners discuss exactly the workflows you care about. |
| **European / UK / US events** | ACT Annual Conference (Liverpool, May)[](cite:act-annual-conference-2026); EuroFinance International Treasury Management (Barcelona, Sept)[](cite:eurofinance-2026); AFP Annual Conference (US, Nov)[](cite:afp-conference-2026). EACT lists national associations in ~26 European countries[](cite:eact-nta) | Large and heavily sponsored by banks and vendors — practitioners are there, but so are hundreds of salespeople. Expect wariness. |
| **LinkedIn** | Search by title + company size + region | Works best with a specific, short, non-sales message (template below). Analysts and managers reply more often than Group Treasurers (field note). |
| **Treasury consultants, ex-treasurers** | Advisers, interim treasurers, TMS implementers | Excellent for vocabulary and cross-company patterns; weaker as evidence of any one company's workflow. Good first conversations. |

<Callout type="warning" title="Treasurers are pitched constantly">

Banks, TMS vendors, FX platforms, forecasting tools and consultants all target the same small population, and conference sponsorship is dominated by them. A message that smells like a sales sequence ("quick 15-minute call to show you…") is ignored. What works (field note): a specific reason you chose *them*, an explicit **no-pitch** promise that you keep, a narrow topic, and something in return — for example a short anonymised summary of what you learned across interviews.

</Callout>

### An approach message that respects that

> Subject: 30 minutes on how your weekly cash forecast gets built?
>
> Hello Ms Keller — I'm a software engineer researching how mid-sized industrial groups in Switzerland run treasury work day to day. I saw your talk at the Swiss Treasury Summit on centralising treasury after an acquisition.
>
> I'm not selling anything and won't demo anything. I'd like to understand how the weekly forecast is actually produced — who sends what, which systems, where it's slow — from someone who does it. No confidential numbers, bank details or names needed; I'm happy to sign an NDA or follow your company's rules.
>
> In return I'll send you a two-page anonymised summary of what I hear across ~20 treasury teams. Would 30 minutes in the next two weeks work?

Why it works: it names a workflow (so they can judge whether they are the right person), states what you **won't** ask for, and gives a reason to say yes. Adjust the workflow to the person's role — see [Who should I interview?](/org/interview-finder).

## Confidentiality and fraud sensitivity

Treasury people handle bank details, payment controls and market-sensitive information. They are also trained — by their banks, auditors and national cyber authorities — to treat unsolicited questions about payment processes as possible fraud reconnaissance. The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) received 971 CEO-fraud reports in 2025, up from 719 in 2024, and advises verifying unusual requests through a second channel[](cite:bacs-ceo-fraud-2026); in the US, three-quarters of organisations surveyed by AFP experienced attempted or actual payments fraud in 2025[](cite:afp-pfc-2026).

**Never ask for:**

- bank account numbers, bank names per entity, balances, facility amounts not already public;
- the specifics of a current control: approval thresholds, who the signatories are, call-back procedures, which system checks what;
- names of counterparties, customers or suppliers;
- documents, screenshots or exports — unless they offer, and even then prefer that they describe rather than send;
- details of a fraud loss.

**Instead ask for** the *shape* of the work ("roughly how many bank accounts — tens or hundreds?"), the *sequence* ("what happens next?"), and *past stories* with the sensitive parts left out ("without naming anyone, what happened the last time a supplier changed bank details?").

<Callout type="field" title="Why a treasurer may need permission to talk to you">

In listed companies and many private groups, employees need approval to speak externally about internal processes, and treasury is closer than most to inside information (financing plans, ratings, M&A funding). Some will clear a conversation with their manager, communications or compliance first; some will only talk in a personal capacity about "companies like ours". Offer an NDA, say you will not quote them by name or company, and accept "I can't talk about that" without pushing. Listed-company treasurers are often more guarded around results publications and financing transactions — do not ask about upcoming refinancings.

</Callout>

## Whom to interview first

Sequencing matters because early interviews teach you the vocabulary and the org chart that later interviews depend on.

<Flow direction="col" steps={["1. Orientation (2–4 conversations)|Ex-treasurers, treasury consultants, TMS implementers: vocabulary, common org patterns, what differs by size", "2. Doers in one segment (6–10)|Treasury analysts and managers at mid-market groups; CFOs / heads of accounting at SMEs: last-instance walk-throughs of one or two workflows", "3. Reviewers and specialists (4–8)|Heads of treasury, cash managers, FX managers, payments leads: what they change, escalate, distrust", "4. Deciders and budget holders (3–5)|CFOs, Group Treasurers: what the output drives, what failure cost, how decisions get made", "5. The other side of the hand-off|Subsidiary finance, AP, accounting, tax: check what treasury told you"]} caption="A typical sequence for the first 20–30 interviews (synthesis)" />

Rules of thumb (synthesis):

1. **Pick a segment and stick to it for a while.** A 70-person SaaS company, a CHF 650m industrial group and a CHF 7bn multinational do different work with different people — see [Treasury by company size](/size). Ten interviews across ten segments teach you less than ten in one.
2. **Doers before deciders.** A Group Treasurer is valuable, hard to reach, and will describe the function at a level of abstraction you can only decode *after* you know the mechanics. Save your best connections for when you can ask sharp questions.
3. **Interview both sides of a hand-off.** After treasury tells you subsidiaries send "bad forecasts", talk to a subsidiary finance manager. After treasury describes the payment run, talk to AP. The gap between the two accounts is often the most useful finding.
4. **Within one company, go wide before deep.** If a treasurer offers introductions, ask for "the person who builds the weekly forecast" and "someone in a subsidiary who submits to it" — see [Who should I interview?](/org/interview-finder).
5. **Revisit.** A second conversation after 20 others lets you test patterns ("others tell me X — is that true here?") without leading on the first.

<Callout type="interview">

The single most useful early question in any treasury interview: *"What were the last three things you worked on?"* It tells you whether this person is a doer, reviewer or decider, which workflows they actually touch, and which part of your guide to use. See [before and after the interview](/interview/before-and-after).

</Callout>
