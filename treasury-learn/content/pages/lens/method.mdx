## Unit of analysis: the assignment

Each of the 20 [canonical workflows](/workflows) is restated as the **assignment** a manager actually gives — e.g.
[covenant monitoring](wf:covenant-monitoring) becomes *"Calculate this quarter's covenants and tell me our headroom for
the next four quarters."* The question for every criterion is then: *could an AI system perform this assignment, or a
meaningful part of it, with the accountable human reviewing?* — not "could AI features help this workflow?"

## The sixteen criteria

<LensCriteria />

Three criteria are **context only** and are coloured grey in the matrix:

- **Current automation level** cuts both ways. High automation means inputs are structured (good) but also that there
  is little left to take over and an incumbent already owns the process (bad). Bank reconciliation is the example.
- **Current human involvement** measures residual human time. High involvement is an opportunity only if that time is
  spent on preparation rather than on accountable decisions.
- Everything else has a direction: for *judgment*, *consequences*, *regulatory* and *trust* a high score is an obstacle
  (marked red in the matrix); for the rest a high score favours an agent.

## How the classification was made

The class is **not a weighted sum**. A workflow can score well on most criteria and still be *hard / dangerous*
because one criterion is decisive — large payment approval is the act the controls exist to reserve for a named human, so
nothing else matters. The rules of thumb applied:

| Class | Typical profile |
|---|---|
| **Good agent candidate** | Frequent (weekly or more) *or* tedious-but-checkable; output verifiable before money moves; a read-only first slice exists; human approval fits existing controls; feedback arrives often. |
| **Possible** | Agent-shaped parts exist, but at least one of: low frequency, weak feedback, heavy judgment, incumbents already automating it, or a small prize. |
| **Hard / dangerous** | The core is an accountable decision or legally reserved act, *or* a rare high-stakes event with no feedback loop. Assistance is fine; "the AI does the job" is not. |

The *good* label says nothing about whether the workflow is a good **business**. Each entry therefore carries a separate
"as a startup opportunity" note, which is often more negative than the agent rating.

## How the scores were produced

1. **Workflow pages first.** Scores draw on the workflow content (frequency, systems, data, judgment points, manual work,
   failure modes) written by the workflow authors, and on the company scenarios in [Company Scenarios](/companies). The
   two gold-standard workflows ([daily cash positioning](wf:daily-cash-positioning), [cash forecasting](wf:cash-forecasting))
   were read in full; the rest were cross-checked when their files existed at the time of writing.
2. **Evidence where it exists.** Surveys (PwC, EACT, AFP, Deloitte) for adoption and priorities; regulatory texts for
   what is legally constrained; vendor announcements for what incumbents claim to be building; academic benchmarks for
   known LLM/agent failure modes. See [the overview](/lens) for the numbers.
3. **Synthesis for the rest.** Most individual scores are the author's judgment. Each has a one-line reason visible in the
   matrix, so a reader can disagree with the reason rather than with a number.

## Limitations — read before quoting a score

<Callout type="warning" title="Known weaknesses">

- **No interviews yet.** None of these ratings has been tested against practitioners. They are the starting hypotheses
  the [interview guide](/interview) is meant to challenge.
- **Size dependence.** A single score hides large differences between [Kleio, Alpine, Helvetic and GlobalChem](/companies).
  Where it matters, notes assume an **international mid-market group** (Helvetic) — the segment the lens thinks is most
  interesting. At a multinational, "current automation" is higher for most cash workflows; at an SME, "frequency" is lower.
- **Ordinal, not interval.** A 4 is more than a 3, not "33% more". Averages across criteria are deliberately not shown.
- **Vendor claims are inputs, not evidence.** Where incumbents announce agents, this lowers *defensibility*; it does not
  raise *suitability*, because the claims are not independently verified.
- **Research on LLM reliability ages quickly.** The benchmarks cited on the overview page used 2023–2024 models. The
  design conclusions (deterministic arithmetic, evidence-linked explanations, repeatability metrics) are more durable than
  the specific error rates.
- **Evidence gaps.** No independent, current study was found that measures accuracy gains from AI cash forecasting or
  agentic positioning in corporate treasury. Absence of evidence is recorded as such, not filled in.

</Callout>

<Callout type="confusion" title="'Automated' vs 'agentic'">

A zero-balancing sweep, a TMS limit check or an auto-match rule in reconciliation is **deterministic automation**: it
does the same thing every time, and nobody calls it AI. An **agent** decides which steps to take, reads unstructured
inputs and produces judgment-like output. Many workflows that look like "AI opportunities" are already solved by the
former — and the latter adds risk without adding value there. See [straight-through processing](term:stp),
[RPA](term:rpa) and [treasury agent](term:treasury-agent).

</Callout>
