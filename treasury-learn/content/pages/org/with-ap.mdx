## Who owns what

| | Accounts payable | Treasury |
|---|---|---|
| **Invoices** | Receives, checks ([three-way match](term:three-way-match)), books, obtains business approval | — |
| **Supplier master data** | Maintains [vendor master data](term:vendor-master-data), incl. bank details (ideally with independent verification) | Sets verification rules in larger groups; investigates fraud |
| **Payment run** | Builds the [payment proposal](term:payment-run) in the ERP by due date and payment terms | Knows the total and timing; funds the accounts |
| **Approval** | First approval of the run | May co-approve large payments; owns signing rules at the bank |
| **Execution** | Sends the file (in many SMEs and mid-market local entities) | Sends it (when treasury or a [payment factory](term:payment-factory) controls bank channels) |
| **Bank connectivity** | User of it | Owns it (e-banking rights, EBICS/SWIFT, multibank tool) |
| **Payment status and rejects** | Re-processes rejected payments | Monitors failed or returned payments in larger groups |

## What AP knows that treasury needs

- **Scheduled payment runs** — date, total, currency, and which accounts they will hit.
- **Large or unusual invoices** — capex milestones, annual licences, customs and duties.
- **Payment behaviour** — which suppliers are paid early (discounts), which late (disputes), which insist on prepayment.
- **Terms changes** — a supplier moving from 60 to 30 days changes the forecast immediately.

AP rarely volunteers this; treasury asks for it. In a 13-week forecast, AP's open items drive the first 2–4 weeks of outflows — see [Cash forecasting](wf:cash-forecasting).

## Who actually executes payments

| Model | Prepares | Approves | Sends to bank | Typical at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Finance does everything** | Accountant / AP | CFO + second signatory ([collective signature](term:collective-signature)) | Same accountant uploads, second approver releases | [Kleio](co:kleio), [Alpine](co:alpine) |
| **Local entities pay** | Local AP | Two local signatories; treasury or CFO co-signs above a threshold | Local finance via local e-banking or group multibank tool | [Helvetic](co:helvetic) |
| **SSC pays** | SSC AP team | Per entity signing matrix in ERP workflow | SSC, through treasury-owned channels | Groups with an SSC but no payment factory |
| **Payment factory** | SSC or local AP | ERP workflow; factory screens and holds exceptions | Payment factory (treasury ops), often [POBO](term:pobo) | [GlobalChem](co:globalchem) in Europe |

<Callout type="confusion" title="Approving an invoice is not approving a payment">

A department head approving an invoice confirms the goods or service were received at the agreed price. A payment approval (signature in e-banking or the payment factory) authorises money to leave the bank. Different people, different controls. Fraud often exploits a gap between the two — a correctly approved invoice paid to a changed bank account.

</Callout>

## Where it breaks

| Break | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Urgent manual payment outside the run ("the supplier will stop delivery") | Bypasses controls; unforecast outflow |
| Supplier bank-detail change accepted by email | Classic [business email compromise](term:business-email-compromise) loss |
| Payment run date moved without telling treasury | Account overdrawn, or funding moved unnecessarily |
| Payment rejected by bank (wrong IBAN, sanctions hit, format) | Late payment; AP and treasury each assume the other handles it |
| Same person can change master data and release payments | Segregation-of-duties failure |

<Callout type="survey" title="Why AP–treasury controls matter">

In the 2025 AFP Payments Fraud and Control Survey (521 mainly US practitioners), 79% of organisations experienced attempted or actual payments fraud and 63% named business email compromise the leading avenue[](cite:afp-pfc-2025). The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) recommends collective signature for payments *and* master-data changes, and verifying change requests via a second channel using a known phone number[](cite:bacs-ceo-fraud-2026).

</Callout>

<Example title="A supplier bank change at Alpine" company="alpine" open>

**Tuesday 10:40.** The AP clerk at [Alpine](co:alpine) receives an email, apparently from a German gearbox supplier, with new bank details "due to a bank merger". Friday's run includes EUR 312k of this supplier's invoices.

**Alpine's rule** (introduced after a near-miss): bank changes are only accepted after a call-back to a number already in the vendor file, and a second person approves the master-data change. The clerk calls the supplier's known number; the supplier knows nothing about a change.

**Who does what next:** the clerk blocks the vendor record; Petra (Head of Accounting) informs Martin (CFO) and checks whether any payment already went to the new account (none); Martin informs the supplier and the IT provider, who finds a compromised mailbox at the supplier. Friday's run goes out to the old, verified account.

**What treasury-type work was involved:** knowing the run total and funding it (Petra), owning the call-back control (Petra/AP), deciding on escalation (Martin). At Alpine these are all "finance"; at [GlobalChem](co:globalchem) the payment factory would additionally flag the changed IBAN before release.

</Example>

<Callout type="interview" title="Questions to ask">

- To AP: "When does treasury hear about your payment run?" "What happens when a supplier changes bank details?"
- To treasury: "Who presses send on a supplier payment here?" "When did an AP payment last surprise you?"
- To both: "Who owns a payment the bank rejected?"

</Callout>
