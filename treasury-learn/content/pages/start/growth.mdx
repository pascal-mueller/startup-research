## The same questions, very different machinery

Whether the company has 30 or 50,000 employees, treasury answers the same questions: *How much cash do we have? How much will we have? Is it enough and in the right place? What risks are we running?* What changes with scale is:

- **Number of legal entities** — each one has its own bank accounts, books, tax position and legal limits on moving cash.
- **Number of banks and accounts** — from 1–2 banks to dozens of banks and hundreds of accounts.
- **Number of currencies** — from one to thirty or more, including some that can't be freely moved.
- **Volume and value of payments** — and therefore the fraud and error risk.
- **Access to financing** — from founder equity and a bank overdraft to bond markets and credit ratings.
- **Who does the work** — from the founder at night to specialised teams in several countries.

<Callout type="synthesis" title="Ranges, not rules">

The tiers below are this manual's synthesis of survey data and typical practice. Real companies vary widely: a 300-person commodity trader can have a more sophisticated treasury than a 3,000-person domestic retailer. Industry, number of countries, and whether the company has debt matter as much as headcount.

</Callout>

## Four snapshots

### 1. A 30–100-person startup

A venture-funded software company with one or two legal entities, a few bank accounts, maybe a USD account for US customers and cloud bills. [Kleio](co:kleio), at 70 people with a Swiss AG and a US subsidiary, 3 banks and 7 accounts, is the manual's example of this tier.

- **Who does treasury:** the founder/CEO or the CFO (at Kleio, Sarah spends about 10% of her time on it); an accountant prepares payments; bookkeeping, statutory accounts or payroll often by an external fiduciary (in Switzerland, a *Treuhänder*).
- **What treasury means here:** [runway](term:cash-runway) ("how many months until we run out?"), making payroll, paying suppliers, and — after raising a round — not leaving CHF 20m sitting in one current account at one bank.
- **Systems:** e-banking portals, cloud accounting software (often connected to the banks through open-banking links), a spreadsheet.
- **Typical decisions:** how to spread the cash across banks and instruments; whether to open an account at a second bank; when to start fundraising; whether to take venture debt. With the SNB rate at 0%, CHF deposits and CHF money-market funds earn about nothing, so the reason to place CHF is **safety and diversification, not yield**; USD balances (for US costs) can earn money-market rates.
- **Typical failure:** runway calculated from the P&L rather than cash (annual prepayments make cash look better than it is for months); concentration at one bank. The March 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank was a sharp lesson for startups that kept all their cash with one institution.

### 2. A 300-person international SME

Like [Alpine Robotics](co:alpine): CHF 50–150m revenue, a parent and two or three foreign subsidiaries, two or three banks, around 10 accounts (Alpine has 9), 2–4 currencies.

- **Who does treasury:** the CFO decides; the head of accounting or a senior accountant does the daily work alongside month-end close, AP and tax.
- **What treasury means here:** a daily look at the bank portals, a weekly cash sheet, funding the subsidiaries, keeping the credit line available, deciding whether to hedge big foreign-currency orders, approving payments, handling bank relationships personally.
- **Systems:** ERP (e.g. Microsoft Dynamics, Abacus, SAP Business One), e-banking portals with file upload (often [EBICS](term:ebics) in Switzerland/Germany), Excel. Usually no [TMS](term:tms).
- **Typical failure:** the weekly forecast lives in one person's spreadsheet; FX is hedged ad hoc when the CFO remembers; signatory lists are out of date.

### 3. A 3,000-person mid-market group

Like [Helvetic Machines](co:helvetic): CHF 300m–2bn revenue, 5–20 subsidiaries in several countries, 4–10 banks, from about 10 to 100 accounts (Helvetic, after cutting its accounts from 31 to 12 in 2024, sits at the low end), 5–10 currencies, a syndicated credit facility with covenants and perhaps a bond.

- **Who does treasury:** a small central team — typically one to five people (a treasury manager or head of treasury plus analysts) reporting to the CFO. Local finance teams in subsidiaries still run their own payments and supply forecast data.
- **What treasury means here:** a real daily cash position across entities, a 13-week forecast collected from subsidiaries, a hedging programme under a written policy, cash concentration via [intercompany loans](term:intercompany-loan) (Helvetic does this manually) or a [cash pool](term:cash-pooling), covenant reporting to banks.
- **Systems:** ERP (often several different ones after acquisitions), a multibank connectivity tool or a lighter cloud TMS, Excel still everywhere.
- **Typical failure:** subsidiary forecasts arrive late or are copied from last month; different ERPs mean no single source of truth; the two-person team has no backup when someone is on holiday.

<Callout type="survey">

The AFP's 2025 benchmarking survey found that US companies with under USD 1bn revenue typically have 1–3 treasury staff, while front- and back-office staff across all respondents average nearly 12 full-time employees[](cite:afp-bench-2025-article). These are US samples of companies that have a treasury function — firms with no treasury staff at all are largely absent.

</Callout>

### 4. A 50,000-person multinational

Revenue in the tens of billions, hundreds of legal entities, 50+ countries, dozens of banks, hundreds or thousands of accounts, 30+ currencies, bonds in several markets, a credit rating, a commercial paper programme. ([GlobalChem](co:globalchem), at CHF 7bn and ~17,000 staff, is a smaller version of this tier.)

- **Who does treasury:** a Group Treasurer with specialised teams — cash & liquidity, FX and interest-rate risk, funding/capital markets, treasury operations (back office), treasury systems — organised so that the people who deal (front office) are not the people who check risk and limits (middle office) or confirm, settle and book (back office): the [front, middle and back office](term:front-middle-back-office) split, often with regional treasury centres (e.g. Singapore, Miami, Dublin) and a payment factory or shared-service centre. Headcount is typically dozens of people; including operations staff in shared services, it can be well over a hundred (field observation, not a survey figure).
- **What treasury means here:** an [in-house bank](term:in-house-bank), automated cash pools and [netting](term:netting), a central payment factory, systematic hedging programmes, capital-markets issuance, rating-agency relationships, and heavy control and audit requirements.
- **Systems:** ERP (often SAP) plus a full TMS, [SWIFT](term:swift) connectivity, trading platforms, market data (Bloomberg/Refinitiv), dedicated forecasting and bank-account-management tools.
- **Typical problems:** forecast quality from dozens of reporting units; restricted-currency countries and [trapped cash](term:trapped-cash); keeping thousands of bank-user rights and signatories under control; integrating acquisitions; system change programmes that take years.

<Callout type="survey">

PwC's 2025 survey of 350 treasurers reports that treasury teams at organisations with more than USD 10bn revenue are investing in in-house banks (67% adoption), payment factories (60%) and payments-on-behalf-of models (50%)[](cite:pwc-gts-2025). These structures are rare below the large-multinational tier.

</Callout>

## When does a company hire its first treasurer?

The four snapshots are static. The interesting moments are the transitions, and they are what CFOs and treasurers talk about ("I was hired when…").

<Callout type="field" title="Common triggers for the first dedicated treasury hire (practitioner pattern, not a survey finding)">

- **The first committed facility with covenants**, especially a syndicated one: someone now has to forecast, report compliance and manage a bank group.
- **The first foreign subsidiaries with their own banks**: cash has to be moved across entities and currencies, and intercompany loans documented.
- **Material FX exposure**: the board wants a written hedging policy and someone to run it.
- **A large fundraise, IPO or bond** leaves a large balance to protect, or brings investor and rating-agency scrutiny.
- **An acquisition**: new banks, new entities, a funds flow on closing day.
- **A lender or board request for a 13-week cash forecast**, often after a scare (a customer default, a covenant near miss).
- **The CFO's time runs out**: cash, banks and FX have been done by the CFO or head of accounting on the side, and something slipped.

Companies differ: some hire a treasury manager at CHF 200m revenue, others run CHF 1bn with a CFO and an accountant. In interviews, ask *"When was the first treasury person hired, and what triggered it?"*

</Callout>

## What changes, in one table

| Dimension | Startup | SME | Mid-market | Multinational |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| People doing treasury | Founder / CFO, part-time | CFO + head of accounting, part-time | 1–5 dedicated | Dozens, specialised, multi-location |
| Legal entities | 1–2 | 2–5 | 5–30 | 100+ |
| Banks | 1–3 | 2–4 | 4–10 | 15–50+ |
| Bank accounts | 2–8 | 5–20 | 10–100 | Hundreds to thousands |
| Currencies | 1–3 | 2–4 | 4–10 | 20–60 |
| Cash visibility | Log in to portals, weekly | Portals daily; spreadsheet on payment days | Daily, via files/connectivity tool | Automated daily + intraday |
| Forecast | Runway model | Weekly cash sheet | 13-week + monthly, from subsidiaries | Multi-horizon, many units, dedicated tool |
| FX management | Convert when needed | Ad hoc forwards | Policy-based hedging programme | Netting + systematic programmes, options |
| Funding | Equity, maybe venture debt | Bank credit line, mortgage | Syndicated RCF, maybe a bond | Bonds, CP, RCF backstop, rating |
| Systems | Portal + accounting app | ERP + portals + Excel | ERP(s) + connectivity/light TMS + Excel | ERP + full TMS + SWIFT + trading platforms |

(Ranges are indicative; see [Treasury by Size](/size) for detail and caveats.)
