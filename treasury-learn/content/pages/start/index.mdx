## The one-paragraph version

Every company has money coming in (customers paying) and going out (suppliers, salaries, taxes, loan interest). Accounting records those flows after they happen. **Treasury is responsible for the money itself**: knowing where it is today, predicting where it will be, making sure there is always enough in the right account, in the right currency, on the right day — and deciding what to do with any surplus or shortfall. Around that core sit the financial risks that come with money: exchange rates, interest rates, the risk that a bank fails, and the risk that a payment goes to a fraudster.

The professional bodies describe it the same way. The UK Association of Corporate Treasurers defines treasury as managing the money and financial risks in a business: first making sure the business has the money it needs for its day-to-day obligations, then funding, risk management and helping shape long-term financial strategy[](cite:act-what-is-treasury). The German treasurers' association's *Guiding Principles of Corporate Treasury* (English edition published through the EACT in 2025) are written to prevent liquidity shortfalls and failures in financial risk management, and treat treasury as part of corporate governance, with firm controls such as segregation of duties[](cite:eact-guiding-principles).

<Callout type="concept" title="The three questions treasury answers every day">

1. **How much cash do we have, and where is it?** [Daily cash positioning](wf:daily-cash-positioning)
2. **How much will we have, and when?** [Cash forecasting](wf:cash-forecasting) and [liquidity planning](wf:liquidity-planning)
3. **What should we do about it?** Borrow or repay, move money between entities, invest, hedge a currency, delay or accelerate a payment.

</Callout>

## Why companies need treasury at all

A company can be profitable on paper and still fail because it runs out of cash on a Tuesday. That gap between profit and cash is what treasury manages.

<Example title="Profitable, growing, and short of cash" company="alpine" open>

[Alpine Robotics](co:alpine) wins three large robot projects in the same quarter. The P&L looks excellent. But Alpine has to buy CHF 4m of components and pay its engineers now, while the customers pay 30% on order, 50% on delivery (four months later) and 20% after acceptance. For about five months, Alpine is funding CHF 3m+ of work out of its own pocket. On top of that, 45% of its revenue is in euros while most of its costs are in Swiss francs (only some components are bought in EUR), so a 5% fall in EUR/CHF would wipe out a meaningful part of the margin.

Nobody at Alpine is called "treasurer". But someone has to see this coming, make sure the [CHF 12m credit line](why:alpine-credit-line) is available, decide whether to lock in the euro rate with a [forward](term:forward), and tell the CEO whether the company can afford a fourth project. At Alpine that is Petra (Head of Accounting), who keeps the cash sheet, and Martin (CFO), who decides. That is treasury work. (Why CHF 12m when the gap above is CHF 3–4m? Hover the line.)

</Example>

The same needs show up in every company, only at different scale:

| Need | Why it exists | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Never miss a payment** | Salaries, suppliers, tax and loan payments have hard dates | Daily balance checks, a [13-week forecast](term:short-term-forecast), a minimum cash buffer, a committed [credit line](term:rcf) |
| **Don't leave cash idle or stranded** | Cash scattered across many accounts earns little, can't be used where needed, and may sit next to expensive debt | [Cash concentration](term:cash-concentration), [cash pooling](term:cash-pooling), [intercompany loans](term:intercompany-loan), repaying drawn debt, deposits or [money market funds](term:money-market-fund) |
| **Borrow at the right time and price** | Growth, acquisitions and bad years need funding; banks lend more readily before you are desperate | Credit facilities, bonds, [covenant](term:covenant) monitoring, bank relationships |
| **Control financial risk** | Currency and interest-rate moves can erase margins; banks can fail | [FX hedging](wf:fx-hedging), interest-rate hedging, [counterparty limits](term:counterparty-limit) |
| **Move money safely** | Payments are the main target of fraud and error | Approval workflows, [signatory](term:signatory) control, bank connectivity, [reconciliation](term:reconciliation) |

## The treasury loop

Most of what treasury does is one loop, repeated daily, weekly and monthly at different horizons:

<Flow direction="col" steps={["Company operates|sells, buys, pays staff", "Money enters / leaves|at banks, by value date", "Treasury observes|balances, flows, exposures", "Treasury forecasts|days, weeks, months", "Treasury decides|fund, repay, invest, hedge, borrow", "Treasury executes|transfers, deals, payments", "Treasury monitors & reconciles|did it happen as planned?"]} loop="Differences between forecast and actual feed the next forecast" caption="The treasury loop. Each workflow in this manual is one pass through some part of this loop." />

The loop runs at several speeds at once:

- **Intraday / daily** — cash positioning, funding accounts before bank [cut-off times](term:cut-off-time), releasing payments. (Minutes and hours matter.)
- **Weekly** — updating the short-term forecast, planning the next week's funding, FX hedges for the coming weeks.
- **Monthly / quarterly** — reporting to the CFO and board, [covenant](term:covenant) calculations, credit-facility rollovers, hedge programme reviews, intercompany settlements, bank fee reviews.
- **Yearly and event-driven** — refinancing, rating reviews, policy updates, acquisitions, crises.

## What does the person actually do?

Abstract descriptions like "treasury optimises liquidity" are unhelpful. Here is one real-feeling morning at [Helvetic Machines](co:helvetic) (CHF 650m revenue, 12 bank accounts at 5 banks, two people in treasury). **Lea**, the Treasury Analyst, builds the cash position; **Daniel**, the Treasury Manager, reviews it and decides. The same morning is worked through in full in the [daily cash positioning](wf:daily-cash-positioning) workflow.

<Callout type="actually" title="Tuesday 17 November, 07:30–10:30 at Helvetic Machines">

1. **07:30, Lea.** Opens the multibank connectivity tool: 11 of 12 prior-day [bank statements](term:bank-statement) are in. The twelfth, HM Suzhou's account at a Chinese domestic bank, is never in the tool, because that bank has no EBICS or Swift reporting to the group; Wei in Suzhou emails a portal export, and Lea types it in. (Time zones are not the problem: the Chinese business day ended while Switzerland slept.)
2. **07:45, Lea.** Refreshes the positioning workbook. Group cash is about CHF 41m, CHF 2.6m below yesterday's forecast.
3. **08:10, Lea.** Traces the gap: HM Italia's largest customer did not pay EUR 2.25m due yesterday (she emails Giulia, the Italian finance manager); the German municipality collected a EUR 0.3m trade-tax prepayment by [direct debit](term:direct-debit) that nobody had forecast; and a CHF 180k supplier payment went out twice. She checks the duplicate against the supplier's bank details before calling it an error rather than fraud.
4. **08:40, Lea.** HM Deutschland will be EUR 1.35m below its account minimum after today's supplier run. She proposes a same-day EUR 1.5m transfer from the parent's EUR account, as a drawing under the existing intercompany framework agreement (priced at arm's length, so no new contract is needed).
5. **09:10, Daniel.** Reads Lea's one-page position email and approves the transfer as second signatory before the bank's early-afternoon EUR cut-off. He decides *not* to do anything with the CHF 13m on the parent's main account: it is needed for the 25 November Swiss payroll and the December payrolls with 13th salaries — the 13-week forecast's [low point](why:week-four-trough) falls in the week of 14 December — and the CFO's CHF 10m minimum liquidity is held at the parent. A CHF deposit would earn about 0% anyway, and the drawn credit facility cannot be prepaid mid-period without break costs, so a reduction waits for the next [interest-period](term:interest-period) rollover.
6. **09:30, Daniel.** Checks the [13-week forecast](term:short-term-forecast): the low point — about CHF 18m in mid-December, about CHF 12m of it usable once the trapped Chinese cash is excluded — stays above the CHF 10m minimum liquidity the CFO wants. He checks the EUR [hedge ratio](term:hedge-ratio) report Lea prepared yesterday. Q4 2026, the nearest quarter, is 25 of EUR 34m hedged — 74%, inside its 60–90% band and on the 75% target. Q1 2027 is 30 of EUR 40m hedged — 75%, which is *above* the 40–70% band that applies to that quarter ([a passive over-hedge](why:passive-over-hedge), since Q1 orders landed after the forwards did; the bands are graded by quarter — 60–90 / 40–70 / 25–55 / 0–40 — and only the nearest quarter gets 60–90%). No new hedges today: per policy the fix is a rebalance within the month — roll about EUR 3m of the Q1 forwards to Q2 at the next hedge run, taking Q1 to about 68% and Q2 to about 51%.
7. **10:00, Daniel.** [Approves (as second signatory)](why:prepares-never-releases) a batch of 64 supplier payments prepared by the Swiss accounting team, after asking AP to call back one supplier whose bank details changed last week.

</Callout>

Every item in that list is a workflow in this manual. Notice who does what: the analyst collects, explains and proposes; the manager decides and co-signs; the CFO does not appear at all unless something is material. When Lea is on holiday, Daniel does all of it himself — the key-person risk that small treasury teams live with.

## Why hold cash while borrowing?

A beginner looking at Helvetic's balance sheet asks: the group has about CHF 41m of cash, about CHF 63m drawn on its credit facility (CHF 30m and EUR 35m) and a CHF 100m bond. Why not use the cash to repay debt? Treasurers do net the two where they can, but gross cash never falls to zero, for concrete reasons:

- **Cash sits where it can't be used.** About CHF 6m is in China, where dividends can be paid only once a year and capital controls apply ([trapped cash](term:trapped-cash)).
- **Every account needs an operating float.** Twelve accounts in six currencies each need a [minimum operating balance](term:minimum-operating-balance) for local payments and direct debits, and the CFO wants CHF 10m at the parent at all times.
- **Cash swings inside the month.** Payroll weeks, VAT dates and supplier runs move group cash by CHF 15–20m within days; the monthly average hides the troughs.
- **Debt can't be repaid at any moment.** The bond is fixed until 2028. Credit-facility drawings run for [interest periods](term:interest-period) of one to three months (rolling on the 15th) and are normally reduced at the rollover dates: mid-period prepayment is possible, but it breaks the bank's funding for the period and carries break costs.
- **Currencies don't match.** EUR cash at the parent is not CHF; converting it to repay a CHF drawing creates FX trades and may leave the EUR side short next week.

What treasurers *do* is keep gross cash as low as safety allows and treat the **undrawn** part of the committed facility (about CHF 137m at Helvetic) as the real liquidity reserve. Surplus CHF goes to reducing drawings at the next rollover, not into deposits.

<Callout type="synthesis" title="What a 0% Swiss franc does to treasury decisions (autumn 2026)">

The SNB left its policy rate at 0% on 24 September 2026[](cite:snb-mpa-2026-09-primary); SARON is around zero. In practice this means:

- **CHF surplus earns about nothing.** A CHF term deposit or CHF money-market fund yields roughly zero, so the motive for placing CHF is safety (spreading cash across banks after the 2023 SVB and Credit Suisse episodes), not income.
- **Repaying debt beats depositing.** A company with drawn floating-rate CHF debt saves the loan margin by reducing the drawing; a deposit earns nothing.
- **Other currencies still pay.** EUR balances earn around €STR (about 2.2–2.4%) and USD balances around SOFR (about 3.5–4%), illustrative. That tempts companies to hold more foreign currency than they need, which is an FX risk, not a free yield.

Treasurers still follow the SLY order for any placement: **safety, liquidity, then yield** (see [investment policy](term:investment-policy)).

</Callout>

## What treasury is *not*

Beginners tend to lump treasury together with everything else in finance. The boundaries matter in interviews, because asking a treasurer about budget variances or tax provisions signals you don't know the territory:

- It is **not accounting**. Accounting records transactions (accruals, the general ledger, financial statements). Treasury manages the real cash and financial risk. They share data constantly — see [Treasury and its neighbours](/start/neighbours).
- It is **not FP&A or controlling**. They forecast and steer revenue, cost and profit; treasury forecasts *cash timing*. Related, but not the same forecast.
- It is **not investment management** in the asset-manager sense. Corporate treasurers place surplus cash under an [investment policy](term:investment-policy) that ranks safety first, liquidity second and yield last. Yield matters, but a treasurer who takes credit or liquidity risk for a few basis points is breaking the policy, not showing skill.
- It is **not trading for profit**. Corporate hedging exists to reduce risk arising from the business. Speculative positions are normally forbidden by the [treasury policy](term:treasury-policy).

<Callout type="survey" title="What treasurers say they prioritise">

In the AFP's 2025 Treasury Benchmarking Survey (523 US practitioners, autumn 2024), cash management and forecasting were the top departmental priority for 73%[](cite:afp-top-tasks-2025), and 62% named cash or liquidity forecasting their most challenging task[](cite:afp-bench-2025). In the 2026 edition (425 responses, May 2026) forecasting was still the most-named challenge — named by about half of respondents (49%, as reported by AFP — figure not confirmed against the primary text)[](cite:afp-bench-2026). The EACT's 2025 survey of European group treasurers ranked cash-flow forecasting as priority number one, ahead of long-term funding and treasury technology[](cite:eact-2025). PwC's 2025 survey of 350 treasurers found CFOs and treasurers ranking cash and liquidity management as a top priority[](cite:pwc-gts-2025).

These are surveys of treasury professionals, mostly at mid-sized and large companies, and the AFP samples are US-based. They tell you what practitioners worry about, not what small companies without a treasurer experience.

</Callout>
